Redesigning TruScope's activation experience, bringing two construction products together, and using AI to simplify document-heavy work

I joined TidalHaus as the sole designer to improve TruScope, an AR application that helped construction teams visualize 3D models on-site and identify issues. The product was struggling to turn downloads into active users and paying customers, and its engineer-built experience needed more than a visual refresh.

Midway through the redesign, the business expanded into construction sheet management. My work grew from improving activation to defining how two very different products could coexist within one application—and finding ways to make the new experience worth switching to. That led to AI-assisted workflows for organizing sheets and turning project documents into tasks and inspections, with user control built into the process.

Impact

Improving conversion while building a more coherent, capable product

The onboarding redesign addressed friction before users could experience TruScope's value. In that initial phase, account creation increased by 58%.

The broader product redesign delivered measurable improvements across conversion and retention, compared with the pre-redesign baseline.

These results reflect the combined impact of improvements across the product, rather than any individual feature.

The impact went beyond the numbers. TruScope gained a shared structure for its AR and sheet-management experiences, a guided sheet-upload workflow shaped by usability testing and engineering constraints, and an AI-assisted way to bring existing project requirements into the product without making users recreate them by hand.

What this case shows

This case shows how I work when the problem changes while the product is being built. I started with an adoption problem, separated surface-level UI issues from deeper experience friction, and then helped turn a business pivot into a coherent product direction across iOS, iPadOS, and web.

It also shows the decisions behind the execution: using competitive research and moderated testing to identify opportunities, aligning with engineering on cost and reliability trade-offs, designing clear error and loading states, and preserving human judgment in AI-assisted workflows. My contribution extended from product strategy and cross-functional alignment to interaction design, visual refinement, and functional prototyping.

The problem

The original TruScope application had a substantial activation problem. 72% of users dropped off between download and account creation, and roughly 0.8% converted into paying customers. Leadership suspected that confusing workflows and an outdated interface were part of the problem, but we needed to understand where the friction actually occurred.

As the redesign neared completion, TidalHaus decided to enter the construction sheet-management market. The original AR experience focused on visualizing models and working in the field. Sheet management was organized around drawings, annotations, documents, and administrative workflows. Leadership wanted the new experience to feel familiar to users of established competitors while maintaining TruScope's identity.

That created two connected challenges. First, the products had to feel like one application without forcing their fundamentally different workflows into the same visual and interaction patterns. Second, familiarity alone wasn't enough to persuade teams to switch. We needed to identify something valuable the existing tools still made difficult.

What I did

I addressed activation before treating the project as a visual refresh

I began with a heuristic evaluation that separated the friction into two groups. Before entering the app, users faced lengthy registration, no SSO, and too much information requested before they could try the product. Once inside, they encountered usability problems in core workflows and an inconsistent interface.

I prioritized reducing barriers to account creation while redesigning the broader experience. That early work was followed by a 58% increase in account creation—a meaningful improvement in the part of the funnel where the initial analysis had identified the clearest friction.

I gave two different products a shared structure

When TruScope expanded into sheet management, I explored how other platforms housed distinct tools within a single product. The answer wasn't to make the AR and document experiences look identical. It was to make navigation, structure, and the relationships between product areas predictable.

I designed around a shared foundation while allowing each experience to preserve interaction patterns users already understood. That balanced the familiarity the business needed in a new market with continuity for existing TruScope users and a maintainable system for a small team.

I found opportunities competitors were leaving on the table

I mapped user journeys and benchmarked competitors to understand where familiar workflows worked and where they still required unnecessary effort. Two opportunities stood out: improving the sheet-upload process and reducing the manual work of creating tasks and inspections.

The second opportunity came from a client conversation. Construction manuals—sometimes more than 1,000 pages long—already contained tasks, inspections, acceptance criteria, and vendor information. People were reading those documents and rebuilding the same information inside their software. That raised a more useful question than simply adding another feature: Why ask users to recreate work that already exists?

I redesigned sheet uploads around guidance and recovery

In moderated, think-aloud sessions using a competitor's workflow, participants valued automatic extraction of drawing numbers and titles. But correcting errors was difficult: construction PDFs could include hundreds of sheets, while the competitor showed only four extraction results at a time.

Full automation wasn't feasible for our MVP because of processing complexity, latency, and cost. Working with engineering, I designed a guided alternative. Users marked where sheet numbers and titles appeared, and TruScope could reuse those locations on the remaining sheets. They could review extracted values, redraw the selection, and rescan pages when necessary.

Testing exposed another issue: numbers and titles weren't always near one another, so I separated their identification into two steps. Some users initially interpreted the transition as an error. I added concise animations and contextual guidance to make the system's state and the next action clear. I also organized successful and failed extractions separately, making it easier to find and correct exceptions.

I turned project manuals into actionable work

I designed a connected workflow that used project documents as a starting point instead of asking teams to enter every requirement manually. TruScope extracts vendor information and classifies vendors as Sole Source, Multi-source, or Preferred; identifies tasks, locations, trades, and specification references; and associates required inspections with the relevant tasks and vendors.

The workflow also checks which vendors are already on the project, supports inviting those who aren't, and lets users review generated tasks and inspections before placing them on construction sheets. The design challenge was less about generating a large amount of information and more about sequencing it so people could understand, validate, and act on it.

I designed AI-assisted work for uncertainty, not perfect output

Document formats differed between construction companies, and engineering raised concerns about unreliable extraction and ambiguous results. Instead of making automation mandatory or silently guessing, I kept steps optional, surfaced processing status, and made outcomes available for review and correction.

When a document pointed to multiple potential vendor matches, for example, the interface presented those choices to the user. That kept the process moving without hiding uncertainty or taking consequential decisions out of people's hands.

I used AI to broaden exploration while keeping ownership of the craft

I used Claude Design to explore alternate flows, information hierarchies, and edge cases; ChatGPT and Figma Make to investigate realistic content and error and loading states; and an early version of UI Forge with Codex to generate Storybook components for functional prototyping. I evaluated the outputs, selected what was useful, and refined the actual experience in Figma. The tools accelerated exploration, but they didn't replace design judgment or final visual execution.

The tradeoff I made

Consistency versus familiarity. Forcing both products into one visual language would have compromised either the original AR experience or the document patterns customers already recognized. I prioritized a shared structure, navigation, and product logic while letting the experiences remain visually and behaviorally distinct where that helped users.

Automation versus reliability. Fully automated sheet extraction sounded more impressive, but it wasn't a practical MVP within our engineering constraints. Guided extraction, reviewable results, optional steps, and explicit choices for ambiguous AI output made the experience more understandable and controllable. I chose the level of automation the product could support responsibly rather than the largest promise we could make.

Learnings

The most useful automation removes repetitive work without removing a person's ability to understand and correct the result. In a document-heavy construction workflow, trust comes from visible progress, clear exceptions, and meaningful control—not from presenting AI output as infallible.

This project also reinforced that product coherence is not the same as visual uniformity. Two different experiences can belong together when navigation and underlying logic are predictable. Designing within business and technical constraints forced me to make those distinctions explicit, and led to solutions that could actually ship.
