Situation

The capability shipped before the experience did

In 2024, Copilot Studio gained GPT-powered natural language understanding. Any company could now build an AI agent that reasoned over its own product catalog, tickets, and internal documents. The power was real. The way to build one was not designed yet.

And the people who most needed these agents could not build software. A sales enablement manager, an HR partner, a procurement lead: each knew exactly what they wanted an agent to do, but getting one meant filing a request with a developer and waiting in a backlog while their knowledge got translated secondhand. My job was to move the build into the hands of the person who owns the problem.

Challenges

One builder, scattered knowledge, and rules that cannot be broken

  • Mixed expertise - The same flow had to carry a first-timer and a power user without slowing either down.
  • Fragmented sources - Answers lived across Teams, SharePoint, internal wikis, and support tools that did not talk to each other.
  • No-code, not underpowered - Hiding the complexity could not mean hiding the capability.
  • Governance - Region, seniority, and sensitivity all constrained what an agent could surface, and some content could never appear at all.
DESIGN PRINCIPLE

You describe the agent. The tool builds it.

Configuration is where these products lose non-technical people. So the flow leads with a conversation: you say what you want the agent to do, in plain language, and the setup follows from that. Configuration stays available for anyone who wants it, but it is the second door, not the first.

The hard call

The two goals fought each other. A builder friendly enough for a non-technical person is also a builder that makes it easy to ship something that leaks, and every user I designed for had material that must never surface. The move most teams reach for is a simpler configuration screen. I argued for removing configuration as the entry point entirely, and for carrying the permission rules on the builder’s behalf, so the easy path and the safe path became the same path. That decision is the design.

Approach

One principle, applied across the build

  • Describe first - The opening screen asks what the agent should do and drafts its name, description, and instructions from the answer. You refine by talking, not by filling forms. Recognition instead of recall.
  • Configuration as the second door - Configure stays one tab away, and power users go straight there. The complexity is deferred until someone asks for it, never removed.
  • Held the line on safety - I kept the permission-aware sourcing and the visible-limits warning even where they added friction, because a builder that hides what an agent cannot see trades a moment of comfort for a wrong answer in front of a customer.
Result

Shipped, and it opened the door to more builders

The builder shipped inside Microsoft 365 Copilot. With the describe-first entry point, non-technical people started creating their own agents that used to sit in a developer's queue, and the number of custom agents created rose. The clearer submission and approval flow made approvals measurably faster, and because governance was built into the flow, that speed did not come at the cost of exposing the wrong thing.
