What This Project Shows

How I challenged a redesign brief, aligned distributed teams, and simplified agent creation

Evidence over assumptions — I challenged a proposed full redesign through competitive benchmarking. The research showed we could reduce friction without replacing Copilot's existing creation model.

End-to-end thinking across teams — I reconstructed the creation journey from work distributed across teams, exposing gaps, outdated patterns, and unclear ownership that were difficult to see in isolation.

Prioritization around creator success — I connected those gaps to first-time activation, completion, and time to first agent, helping teams adjust priorities and responsibilities.

Hands-on design with system-level influence — I redesigned Configure around progressive setup and permission-aware knowledge selection, while extending the journey-mapping approach to other Copilot JTBD.

Impact

Changing product direction, shipping a lighter creation flow, and improving alignment across teams

Product direction — Competitive evidence shifted the discussion from replacing Describe and Configure to targeted improvements within the existing interaction model.

Shipped experience — The revised Configure experience reached production. A repeated benchmark showed fewer required interactions to create an agent, while preserving options for advanced configuration.

Cross-team alignment — A shared end-to-end journey exposed overlooked experience gaps and unclear ownership, contributing to changed priorities and redistributed work.

Organizational reuse — After a positive response from leadership, I applied the same journey-mapping and asynchronous review process to other major Copilot JTBD.

The exact benchmark figures are confidential. Fewer required interactions were verified; an increase in agent creation, completion, or adoption was not established with the available post-launch instrumentation.

1. The brief assumed the flow was broken

Agent Builder offered two connected ways to create an agent. In Describe, creators explained what they wanted through a conversation. In Configure, they inspected and edited the same agent through structured fields. They could switch between the two without starting over.

Leadership wanted a simpler experience and proposed a complete redesign. But replacing the model would have been an expensive response to an unresolved question: Was the interaction model the problem, or was there simply too much work before someone could try an agent?

I started by testing that assumption instead of designing replacement screens.

2. Benchmarking changed what we decided to build

I compared agent-creation journeys across ChatGPT, Gemini, Amazon, Salesforce, and Copilot, looking at time to create, required interactions, information presented, and inputs requested.

Two patterns emerged. Express builders reduced early decisions through inference, defaults, and simpler starting points. Advanced builders exposed more configuration in exchange for greater control.

Copilot already fit the express category, but asked for more effort than the lightest alternatives. Its core Describe/Configure model was useful; the opportunity was to remove unnecessary setup within it.

Decision: Keep the connected creation model and focus on the interactions delaying a creator's first useful agent. Other designers worked on Describe; my hands-on design responsibility was Configure.

3. The bigger blind spot was between teams

The benchmark clarified the direction, but prioritizing improvements required seeing the full journey. Different teams owned different steps and components, worked across time zones, and sometimes held different versions of the latest designs. No one had assembled a current end-to-end picture of what a creator actually experienced.

I used Custom Agent Creation as the organizing JTBD. I contacted the teams involved, collected their latest screens and flows, and reconstructed the journey in sequence.

Putting the work together revealed outdated components, inconsistencies between steps, and handoffs whose ownership had become unclear. I invited the contributing teams to comment asynchronously, identify constraints, and propose changes directly on the shared map.

The map became more than a design inventory. It gave teams a shared basis for deciding what needed attention and who should own it. Some work was redistributed, and priorities changed to cover previously overlooked gaps.

4. From gaps to decisions tied to creator success

A shared map showed us the problems, but not which ones mattered most. I used three KPIs to connect the gaps to the original adoption goal:

KPIPrioritization question
First-time creator activationWhat could prevent someone from reaching a first meaningful agent experience?
Creation completion rateWhere could someone start but fail to finish?
Time to first agentWhat delayed getting an agent ready to try?

I assessed which outcomes each gap might affect and prioritized problems in the context of the complete journey—not by the visibility of an individual component or team.

Experience problemDecisionIntended benefit
Too much setup before an agent could be triedInfer, default, or defer configuration wherever appropriateShorter path to a first agent
Inconsistent handoffs and unclear ownershipMake gaps and owners explicit in the shared journeyMore coherent experience across teams
Knowledge sources with restricted accessSurface an access warning during source selectionFewer surprises when sharing agents

The first decision removed work. The third deliberately retained an interruption because hiding the issue could produce a more serious failure later.

These KPIs guided prioritization; they were not verified post-launch improvements.

5. Make the first agent easy—and the next edit possible

In Configure, I shifted the experience from finish configuring, then try to create, try, then refine.

Previous pathRevised path
Supply configuration up frontDescribe what the agent should do
Complete setup before trying itGenerate and try an initial agent
Return to configuration to make changesRefine settings when the need becomes clear

The initial description supplied the creator's intent. The experience could infer details already present in that description, apply reasonable defaults, and leave specialized settings for later.

Advanced control remained available in Configure: creators could inspect and edit identity, instructions, knowledge sources, starter prompts, and supported capabilities. Describe and Configure continued to work with the same underlying agent information, so people could move between conversation and structured editing.

The design trade-off was to reduce what beginners had to decide without taking control away from experienced creators. The first useful result needed to be accessible, but also understandable and editable.

6. The place where simplification needed a limit

Knowledge selection introduced a different kind of friction. I designed a deeper OneDrive integration and a custom way to explore organizational files. Those sources carried permission rules that did not always match the audience an agent would eventually serve.

For example, a creator might test an agent using a restricted OneDrive file and see it work. Someone receiving that agent without access to the file could have a different experience.

The shortest flow would ignore that risk until sharing. Instead, I chose to show an access warning during source selection, while the creator could still act on the information. The interface explained that people without access might not receive the full experience, and let creators consider the file's existing access model.

That warning introduced a small interruption. But reducing clicks was not worth hiding a limitation that could undermine trust in a shared agent.

Design principle: Remove unnecessary decisions, but keep decisions that determine whether the agent will work for its audience.

7. What changed—and what we could prove

The revised Configure experience shipped, and repeating the original benchmark showed fewer required interactions to create an agent. The precise figures are confidential. This supports a narrower conclusion than the original business goal: the journey became lighter, but the benchmark alone does not prove that more people created agents.

The project also changed how teams worked together. Shared journey reviews surfaced neglected gaps, prompted adjustments in ownership and priorities, and provided an approach I later applied to other Copilot JTBD.

We did not have sufficient post-launch instrumentation to confirm improvements in first-time creator activation, completion, or time to first agent. Those would be the first outcomes I'd measure next, alongside source-access problems, post-generation editing, and continued use.

What I learned

The most important contribution wasn't simply making Configure shorter. It was recognizing what didn't need to be redesigned, bringing an experience fragmented across teams into one shared view, and using that perspective to make better product decisions.

Simplification also has limits. The best experience removes work that doesn't help the user while keeping critical choices—and their consequences—visible.
