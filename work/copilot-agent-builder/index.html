What This Project Shows

How I challenged a redesign brief, aligned distributed teams, and simplified agent creation

Evidence over assumptions — I challenged the original full-redesign brief through competitive benchmarking, showing that Copilot's existing creation model needed targeted improvements, not replacement.

End-to-end thinking across teams — I reconstructed the creation journey from work spread across globally distributed teams, exposing experience gaps, outdated patterns, and unclear ownership.

Prioritization tied to product goals — I connected journey gaps to creator activation, completion, and time to first agent, helping teams realign priorities and responsibilities.

Simplification without losing control — I redesigned Configure around progressive setup and permission-aware knowledge selection, then extended the journey-mapping approach to other major Copilot JTBD.

Impact

A simpler path to creating custom agents, and a shared approach to improving the experience across Copilot teams

FEWER
Required interactions to create an agent, confirmed through a repeat benchmark
MULTIPLE

The redesigned experience shipped. Exact benchmark figures are confidential. Post-launch adoption and completion improvements could not be verified with the available instrumentation.

1. The brief assumed the flow was broken

Agent Builder offered two connected ways to create an agent. In Describe, creators could explain what they wanted in a conversation. In Configure, they could inspect and edit the same agent through structured fields. They could move between the two without starting over.

Leadership wanted a simpler experience and proposed a complete redesign. But replacing the model would have been an expensive answer to a question we had not yet settled: was the interaction model the problem, or was the amount of work required before someone could use it?

I started with that distinction rather than with new screens.

2. Benchmarking changed what we decided to build

I compared the end-to-end creation journeys in ChatGPT, Gemini, Amazon, Salesforce, and Copilot. I looked at four dimensions: time to create an agent, number of interactions, information presented to the creator, and inputs the creator had to supply.

Two patterns emerged. Express builders reduced early decisions through inference, defaults, and simpler starting points. Advanced builders exposed more configuration in return for greater control. Copilot already fit the express category; it was simply heavier than the lightest examples.

That finding challenged the requested full redesign. The basic combination of conversation and structured configuration made sense. The more consequential opportunity was to reduce setup cost inside that model.

Decision: Retain the connected Describe/Configure model and focus investment on the interactions delaying a creator's first useful agent. Other designers evolved Describe; my hands-on scope was Configure.

3. The bigger blind spot was between teams

The benchmark established a direction, but prioritizing improvements required seeing the entire experience. That was harder than expected. Different teams owned different steps and components, worked across time zones, and held different versions of the latest designs. Individual pieces were progressing, but no one had assembled a current picture of what a creator actually encountered from beginning to end.

I used Custom Agent Creation as the organizing JTBD and contacted the teams responsible for each part. I gathered their current flows, screens, and components, then reconstructed the journey in sequence. Once the work was visible together, so were gaps that had been hidden by team boundaries: legacy or outdated patterns, inconsistencies between steps, and handoffs that had changed owners so often that responsibility was unclear.

Rather than conducting a review that ended with my assessment, I invited everyone involved to comment on the shared map asynchronously. Teams could flag problems, explain constraints, and propose changes in context. It worked as a distributed design review: a way to build a shared understanding without requiring everyone to join the same meeting or defend isolated components.

This changed the artifact's purpose. It was no longer just a map of the journey; it became a common basis for deciding which gaps mattered and who should resolve them. Some work was redistributed and some priorities were adjusted to cover gaps that had previously been overlooked.

4. From a collection of gaps to a sequence of decisions

A shared map made problems visible, but it did not automatically tell us what to fix first. I defined three KPIs around the original adoption objective and used them to assess the likely effect of the gaps we found:

KPIThe question it helped us ask
First-time creator activationWhat might prevent a new creator from reaching a first meaningful agent experience?
Creation completion rateWhere might someone who started fail to finish?
Time to first agentWhat delays reaching an agent that can actually be tried?

I associated gaps with the KPI each might affect and considered their likely significance in the full journey, rather than letting the most visible component or the loudest team determine the priority. These were prioritization criteria, not measured post-launch improvements; we lacked the instrumentation to establish their causal effect on creator behavior.

The resulting decisions illustrate why this mattered:

Experience problemDecisionIntended creator benefit
Too many decisions before an agent could be triedMake initial creation possible with a description; infer, default, or defer other configurationShorter path to first agent; fewer potential reasons to abandon setup
Fragmented handoffs and outdated work across team boundariesMake gaps and owners explicit in the shared journey; adjust responsibilities and prioritiesMore coherent experience across steps, not just better individual screens
Restricted knowledge sources could work for a creator but not for their audiencePreserve an explicit access warning during source selectionFewer surprises when an agent is shared, even at the cost of some extra friction

These were not equally about speed. The first decision removed work; the third deliberately retained a decision because hiding it could make the agent less useful for other people. That distinction guided the detailed Configure design.

5. Make the first agent easy—and the next edit possible

The core Configure decision was to move from finish configuring, then try to create, try, then refine.

Previous pathRevised path
Supply configuration up frontDescribe what the agent should do
Complete setup before trying itGenerate and try an initial agent
Return to configuration to make changesRefine settings when the need becomes clear

I reduced the information required at the start. A description supplied the initial intent. The system could infer a name or other details already present in that description, use reasonable defaults for common options, and leave more specialized settings for later.

This did not mean removing advanced configuration. In Configure, creators could still inspect and edit the agent's identity, instructions, knowledge sources, starter prompts, capabilities, and actions or connectors. The conversational Describe experience and structured Configure experience continued to operate on the same underlying agent information, so users could move between them.

The trade-off was deliberate: reduce the amount a beginner must decide without reducing what an experienced creator can control. The interface had to make the first useful result attainable while keeping the system understandable and editable rather than turning AI-generated choices into a black box.

6. The place where simplification needed a limit

Knowledge and external actions introduced a different kind of complexity. I designed a deeper OneDrive integration and a custom explorer for organizational files; the experience also expanded to support connectors to external services.

Consider a creator who builds an agent using a restricted OneDrive file. The creator can access the source and test the agent successfully. But if they share the agent with someone who lacks access, it can behave differently for that person. A creator who only sees a successful test could reasonably assume the shared agent will work the same way for everyone.

The fastest possible flow would defer this issue until after sharing. I chose to surface it while the creator selected a source, at the moment when the information could change their decision. Selecting a restricted file produced a warning that people without access might not receive the full experience. Creators could also use the file's existing access model as the basis for the agent's permissions.

That warning added friction. It was also a necessary counterweight to the push for fewer interactions. Time to first agent was not worth optimizing if doing so concealed a failure that only appeared for the agent's audience.

7. What changed—and what we could not yet prove

The work produced consequences at several levels:

  • Product direction. The benchmark moved the conversation from a full replacement to targeted improvements within the existing Describe/Configure model.
  • Product design. The updated Configure experience shipped with a lighter path to trying an agent, while preserving more advanced editing, organizational knowledge selection, permissions awareness, and external-service capabilities. Other designers owned the changes to Describe.
  • Team coordination. The E2E journey and asynchronous review exposed neglected gaps and unclear ownership, leading to changed priorities and redistributed work.
  • Organizational reuse. Leadership responded positively, and I applied the same journey-mapping approach to Copilot's other major JTBD rather than keeping it confined to Custom Agent Creation.

I repeated the original competitive benchmark after the redesign. It showed fewer required interactions to create an agent; the exact comparison figures are confidential. That is evidence that we lowered the interaction cost of the flow. It is not evidence, on its own, that more people created agents.

We did not have the post-launch instrumentation to verify first-time creator activation, creation completion, or time to first agent, or to attribute adoption changes to these designs. Those KPIs informed the work and would be the first ones I would instrument next, alongside guardrails for source-access failures, post-generation editing, and continued usage.

My central takeaway: The important work was not just simplifying Configure. It was identifying what didn't need to be redesigned, making a fragmented end-to-end experience visible to the people shaping it, and turning that shared view into more coherent decisions—without losing control and trust in the details.
